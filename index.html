Q What did your phone show first?

Before this page opened, your phone showed you the link.

A good scanner (your built-in Camera, Google Lens) decodes the QR and shows the destination as a banner — it waits for you to tap. That pause is the entire defence against quishing (QR phishing). Apps that open links instantly take that pause away.

The attack is simple: a sticker with a malicious QR gets slapped over a real one — on a parking meter, a poster, a restaurant table. The code looks identical. Only the link underneath is different.

Reading a QR link in 4 checks

  • Find the real domain. Read the part just left of the first single slash, right-to-left. In paypal.com.secure-login.ru the real site is secure-login.ru — not PayPal.
  • Watch for lookalikes. paypa1.com, micros0ft.com, or odd characters that look Latin but aren't (a sign: xn-- in the link).
  • Distrust shorteners & raw IPs. bit.ly/xxxx or http://45.9.x.x hide where you're really going. Expand the short link before trusting it.
  • Check the sticker. A QR stuck on crookedly, or a sticker-over-a-sticker, is the classic quishing tell in the real world.

The rule that beats all four

A QR code should never be how you arrive at a login or payment page. If a scan lands you somewhere asking for a password or money, close it and reach the site the way you already know — type it, use a bookmark, or open the official app.
